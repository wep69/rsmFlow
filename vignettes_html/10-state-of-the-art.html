State of the art, ecosystem position, and validation tiers

Scope and gap

rsmFlow does not claim that R lacks classical response-surface methodology. The classical rsm ecosystem already provides established polynomial RSM, coding, CCD/Box-Behnken construction, canonical analysis, steepest ascent and ridge workflows (Lenth 2009). The package gap addressed here is integration: design-quality diagnostics for imperfect/non-orthogonal experiments, classical and generalized response surfaces, nonlinear agronomic surfaces, global optimization, uncertainty of optimum coordinates, near-optimal/economic decisions, and explicit Tier-3 surrogates within one auditable API.

Mixture experiments remain outside scope.

Why generalized response surfaces?

For two quantitative factors, a GLM can retain the second-order response-surface predictor while using a response distribution/link compatible with counts, proportions or positive asymmetric responses. rsmFlow therefore supports Gaussian, Poisson/quasi-Poisson, binomial/quasi-binomial, Gamma, inverse-Gaussian and optional negative-binomial surfaces. Optimization is performed on the response scale by default, and quasi families are not ranked with ordinary likelihood AIC/BIC.

d <- expand.grid(Dose=seq(-1,1,length.out=7),Day=seq(-1,1,length.out=7))

Why nonlinear two-factor surfaces?

Agricultural dose-response literature has explicitly documented cases where conventional second-order polynomial surfaces showed unacceptable lack of fit and where multifactor Mitscherlich/Gompertz alternatives were developed (Landes et al. 1999). Later work continued the comparison of nonlinear dose-response models and experimental-design strategies (Frenzel et al. 2010). This motivates the built-in Mitscherlich, Gompertz, Michaelis-Menten, asymptotic, Hoerl and plateau candidates in rsmFlow, without implying universal superiority.

Tier 3 and sequential experimentation

Tier 3 is deliberately opt-in. It includes generalized nonlinear least squares, Gaussian processes, GAM/thin-plate smooths, random forests, neural networks, and one-step expected-improvement Bayesian optimization. Expected-improvement workflows are rooted in surrogate-based global optimization (Jones et al. 1998). rsm_bayes_opt() proposes the next run only; the package does not invent the response that would be observed at that run.

Workflow example 3: explicit Tier-3 alternative

set.seed(403)
s <- data.frame(x1=runif(70,-1,1),x2=runif(70,-1,1))
s$y <- sin(2*s$x1)+cos(2*s$x2)+rnorm(70,0,.05)
gam_fit <- rsm_surrogate(s,"y",c("x1","x2"),method="gam")
rsm_surrogate_optimize(gam_fit,"max")

Validation tiers

The package uses validation tiers rather than automatic model escalation.

  • Tier 1: classical design-aware Gaussian RSM and design geometry.
  • Tier 2: GLM-RSM, nonlinear response surfaces, GA optimization/initialization, multiple-response and economic decision tools.
  • Tier 3: gnls, GP, GAM/TPS, random forest, neural network and Bayesian next-run recommendation.

A Tier-3 model is not selected solely because it lowers RMSE or AIC. Scientific support, experimental design, convergence, diagnostics, predictive behavior, uncertainty and stability of the operational optimum remain part of the decision.
